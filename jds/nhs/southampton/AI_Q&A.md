## Question 1

Patients First (Trust Values) Tell us about what this means to you and how you feel the role you are applying for links to this


### Answer

Patients First means the technology behind patient care must never get in the way of that care. When a clinical system is slow, confusing, or unavailable, the person who absorbs that cost is the member of staff at the patient's side. Designing and maintaining software so that it is reliable, accessible, and easy to use is not a technical nicety; it is a way of protecting clinical time and, through it, the patient.

Patients First is also an equitable principle. A hospital serves people of every age, background, language, and ability, and those differences are lived realities in the health outcomes they experience. Software that assumes a particular level of digital confidence, a certain language, or a specific device silently widens inequalities in access to care. Patients First therefore means deliberately designing for the people who are most likely to be excluded: patients with visual or motor impairments, non-native speakers, older patients with lower digital confidence, and carers supporting them. When I build, I try to keep that user in mind rather than the average user.

Accessibility is where Patients First and inclusion meet directly. As Mastercard's Accessibility Champion I helped raise WCAG compliance by 40% and contributed fixes to a shared design system. Accessibility work is fundamentally about treating disability and difference as something the service must accommodate rather than something users must compensate for: semantic markup for screen-reader users, visible focus for keyboard users, plain language for people processing under stress or in a hurry. Software that is not accessible excludes users; software that is accessible lets every patient and every member of staff use it on their own terms. That principle maps directly to a Trust that serves whole communities.

This role links to Patients First because the Clinical Scientific Computing service builds the very software that supports patient care across the Trust. Every design decision, test, release, and incident response in this post ultimately lands in front of a clinician caring for a patient. Leading that work with reliability, accessibility, and inclusion at the centre is the most direct way I can put patients first through engineering.

## Question 3

Always Improving (Trust Values) Tell us about how you approach change and give us an example of an improvement you've made from doing things in a different way


### Answer

I approach change by first understanding the current system, then measuring a targeted improvement, then making the change stick through documentation and shared practice by writing papers, documentation and providing sessions to people and teams. I do not change things for their own sake. I look for the bottleneck, find the root cause, fix that, and then make sure the improvement survives by embedding it in how the team works. I also ask who is least served by the way things are done now, because the most valuable improvements are often the ones that make the system work for the people it currently fits worst and improving for the least served usually improves it for everyone.

An example was figuring out why a particular library was not working on a Windows since I had a Windows laptop. Since it worked only on Mac and Linux machines, I tried to figure out the root cause, which led me to install a Linux VM. In spite of high latency, I managed to figure out the root cause in an obscure shell script file in a downstream library which didn't work on Windows. I documented this process and confirmed with a live team that managed the library. This helped the team triage the issue to fix the library to work on Windows, and I was able to resume development and improve bug fixes for that particular library. 
For this role, I would bring that same mindset to the CSC team: look for the process or system that costs the team time, consider who it excludes or burdens most, propose a measured change, and embed it so the improvement lasts. That is what Always Improving means to me.
