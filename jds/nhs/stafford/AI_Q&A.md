## Question 2

Give me an example of a time when you have gone beyond your job role / remit?

What happened/what was the outcome?
How did you know you made a difference?
What do you think made this extra or above and beyond?

### Answer

One example was taking on the role of Accessibility Champion at Mastercard. My core remit was delivering application features and supporting the platform, but accessibility issues affected multiple teams and could not be solved effectively within the boundaries of one product module.

I chose to go beyond my immediate delivery responsibilities because inaccessible patterns become more expensive to correct when they are repeated across applications. Fixing isolated defects would have helped individual screens, but it would not have changed how teams designed and reviewed new work. I therefore treated accessibility as a shared engineering-quality issue rather than a final compliance check.

I worked across more than ten engineering teams to explain accessible implementation patterns and help teams apply them in their own React and TypeScript work. I also contributed five accessibility fixes and additions to the internal design system. This was important because improving shared components made accessible behaviour the easier default for future features instead of requiring every team to solve the same problem independently. I documented the approach in an internal Accessibility whitepaper so the learning remained available beyond individual conversations.

The measurable outcome was a 40% increase in WCAG compliance scores across the product suite. I knew the work had made a difference because the improvement appeared in the compliance results, the shared design system contained concrete fixes that other teams could reuse, and the guidance became internal reference material circulated to 200 engineers.

What made this above and beyond was its scope. I was not only completing an assigned feature or correcting a defect in my team's code. I took ownership of a cross-team problem, coached engineers outside my immediate team, contributed to a shared platform, and created documentation that could continue influencing delivery after the initial work was complete.

The outcome reinforced for me that engineering impact is not limited to the code I personally ship. Changing shared components, review habits, and team understanding can improve many user journeys at once. I found the result rewarding because it combined a clear improvement for users with a lasting improvement in engineering practice. It is the same approach I would bring to MPFT: address the immediate need, then look for reusable patterns, documentation, and knowledge sharing that make the wider service stronger.
